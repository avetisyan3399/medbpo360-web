## Why these three, and only these three

The personal audience is mostly real estate, insurance and finance, with roughly 10% healthcare. Posting billing-modifier content at them weekly would burn a genuinely valuable asset for nothing.

These three have a business angle that lands with a finance-adjacent reader: private equity deal flow, ownership consolidation, and providers beating payers in disputes. The rest of the queue stays on the company page where it belongs.

**Cadence: one a week at most, spaced roughly a week off the company-page version** so the two do not compete and the profile does not read as a company account in disguise.

**Voice:** first person, opens with a personal observation, no "we". The facts are identical to the company posts; the framing is a person with a view.

## A. Private equity deal mix
*Company page runs its version Thu 2026-10-01. Post this around 2026-10-08.*

> Spent the week looking at healthcare deal data and one number stuck with me.
>
> Of 1,029 private equity healthcare transactions tracked in 2025, 664 were add-on acquisitions. Against 151 leveraged buyouts.
>
> That ratio says something specific: the returns aren't really in picking the right practice any more. They're in whether you can integrate the twelfth one as cleanly as the second.
>
> Which makes it an operations problem wearing a finance costume. And the places it breaks are dull and predictable — credentialing started after close instead of when the deal is in motion, so providers are clinically live and unbillable. Every acquired practice keeping its own billing conventions because standardising them was nobody's line item. Reporting that can't compare sites because no two define a metric the same way.
>
> None of that shows up in the first acquisition. All of it shows up by the fifth.
>
> Wrote up where it usually goes wrong → https://medbpo360.com/blog/mso-pe-acquisition-onboarding-billing

## B. Independent practice ownership
*Company page runs its version Thu 2026-09-17. Post this around 2026-09-24.*

> There's a number that gets quoted at doctors constantly, usually by someone selling them an exit.
>
> Physicians in private practice fell from 60.1% in 2012 to 42.2% in 2024, per the AMA's benchmark survey. Real data, real trend, and it gets used to argue that independent practice is finished.
>
> Read it again though. After twelve years of consolidation pressure, 42.2% of physicians are still independent. That's not a vanishing category — it's a large, durable one that has been declared dead for a decade.
>
> What actually changed isn't viability. It's the operational floor. Staying independent now means running systems that larger organisations build deliberately and small ones never build by accident.
>
> I find that a more interesting problem than the consolidation story, and a more useful one.
>
> https://medbpo360.com/blog/independent-practice-consolidation-trends

## C. Providers winning payer disputes
*Company page runs its version Mon 2026-10-05. Post this around 2026-10-12.*

> A number that surprised me, from the federal surprise-billing dispute data.
>
> When providers take a payer to independent dispute resolution, they win 80% of the time. And the win rate climbed from 68% in early 2023 to 85% by early 2024.
>
> Over 1.2 million disputes were filed between January 2023 and June 2024, with providers initiating 90% of them.
>
> The interesting part isn't that the process exists. It's that most groups treat it as a last resort — something you escalate to when everything else fails — when the data says it's a process they usually win.
>
> Which turns the question from "should we dispute this" into "can we file, on time, with the documentation, at volume". A completely different problem, and an operational one.
>
> https://medbpo360.com/blog/hospital-based-groups-out-of-network-disputes
