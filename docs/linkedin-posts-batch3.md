## Why this batch is mixed rather than uniform

Every post so far has been a blog companion: sourced, declarative, ending in a link out. Across eleven posts that format produced **400 impressions, 7 clicks, 0 reactions and 0 comments**. The writing is not the problem — the posts are accurate and useful. But LinkedIn expands reach on early interaction, and nothing here has ever produced any, so nothing ever expands.

All 18 blog posts now have companions, so the supply is exhausted anyway. This batch deliberately breaks format:

- **2 polls** — responding costs one tap, which is the most direct attack on zero engagement
- **2 resource posts** — the lab and credentialing checklists have never had posts of their own, and unlike blog links they capture an email
- **3 re-angles** — existing blog posts led from a different fact
- **1 opinion piece** — no blog dependency at all

If the polls also produce nothing, that is genuinely informative: it would mean reach, not format, is the binding constraint, and the answer is paid distribution rather than better writing.

## 20. Lab billing checklist · Mon 2026-10-12

> Here is a lab billing question with a non-obvious answer.
>
> A molecular test is ordered on a hospital outpatient. The specimen is collected during the encounter. Who bills Medicare — the hospital, or the lab that ran it?
>
> It depends on whether the result guided treatment during that same encounter. If it did, the lab cannot bill Medicare directly. If it did not, and two other conditions hold, it can.
>
> Get that backwards at volume and you have months of claims that were never yours to submit.
>
> That is one of seven places we see diagnostic revenue leak. The others: PAMA reporting, ABN validity, coverage rules that differ by MAC jurisdiction, panel scope, and genetic testing documentation.
>
> Checklist, with the CMS and OIG sources cited throughout → https://medbpo360.com/resources/lab-billing-compliance-checklist

## 21. Re-angle — prior authorisation · Thu 2026-10-15

> 93% of physicians say prior authorisation delays necessary care. That figure is from the AMA's own physician survey, and it gets quoted constantly as an access problem.
>
> It is also a revenue problem, and that part gets discussed less.
>
> Every delayed authorisation is a procedure that slips into a later month, a patient who reschedules, or a claim that denies for an authorisation that expired before the date of service. None of that shows up as a prior auth statistic. It shows up as denials, AR days, and a schedule with holes in it.
>
> In cardiology it is the single largest driver of denials we see — ahead of coding errors, ahead of eligibility.
>
> How the patterns break down by payer → https://medbpo360.com/blog/denial-management-cardiology-payer-patterns

## 23. Re-angle — the parity gap · Thu 2026-10-22

> Federal parity law says behavioural health benefits cannot be managed more restrictively than medical and surgical ones. In practice they frequently have been.
>
> The Department of Labor's most recent MHPAEA report found corrections that benefited more than 7.6 million plan participants. That is the scale of the gap between what the law requires and what was being administered.
>
> For a practice, that lands as denials that look routine and are not. Some behavioural health denials are worth challenging on parity grounds rather than resubmitting with a cleaner claim — but only if someone recognises which ones.
>
> Most billing teams are not looking for that, because it is a legal argument wearing a claims-denial costume.
>
> Where behavioural health denials actually come from → https://medbpo360.com/blog/behavioral-health-billing-denial-rates

## 24. Credentialing timeline checklist · Mon 2026-10-26

> The most expensive date in credentialing is not the one most practices track.
>
> Medicare sets the effective date of billing privileges as the later of the date you filed, or the date the provider first began furnishing services at that location. Retrospective billing reaches back only 30 days.
>
> So a provider who starts seeing Medicare patients sixty days before the application is filed has thirty days of work that cannot be billed to anyone. Not the payer, not the patient. It is simply gone, and usually nobody records it as a loss because nobody was watching that date.
>
> That is one of seven dates worth checking. Revalidation deactivation is another — it runs on Medicare's calendar, to whatever correspondence address is on file, which for a lot of practices is a suite they moved out of.
>
> Checklist, sourced to CMS contractors → https://medbpo360.com/resources/credentialing-timeline-checklist

## 25. Re-angle — the regulatory floor · Thu 2026-10-29

> MGMA's 2026 Regulatory Burden Report surveyed 230+ practice leaders. Nearly 95% said regulatory burden had risen over the past three years. 40% are now hiring multiple full-time administrative staff per physician just to keep up.
>
> Multiple admin staff per physician. That is the actual cost of staying compliant, and it is being absorbed quietly by practices that never budgeted for it.
>
> It also explains something that confuses people about outsourcing decisions: practices rarely outsource because billing got worse. They outsource because the administrative load grew around billing until the same people could not do both.
>
> The tipping point is usually three things at once — nobody auditing coding, AR days climbing without explanation, and the billing staff absorbing prior auth work they did not use to do.
>
> When in-house billing stops making sense → https://medbpo360.com/blog/primary-care-billing-when-to-stop-in-house

## 26. Opinion — the cheapest thing nobody does · Mon 2026-11-02

> The least glamorous number in a practice is the percentage of denials that get worked at all.
>
> Not appealed successfully. Worked. Touched by a human who read the reason code and decided what to do.
>
> In most practices nobody knows what that number is, which is itself the answer. Denials arrive, some get fixed because someone noticed, and the rest age quietly into write-offs at 90 or 120 days. No decision is ever made to abandon them. They just expire.
>
> It is money already earned. The work is already done, the patient was already seen, the claim was already submitted. The only thing standing between the practice and the payment is somebody opening the denial.
>
> If you measure one thing this quarter, measure that percentage. It is usually worse than expected, and it is the cheapest thing on the list to fix.
