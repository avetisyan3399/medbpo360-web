### 1. Cardiology denial patterns

*Pillar: Billing & Denials*

> Cardiology denials aren't random — they cluster.
>
> MGMA benchmarking puts the average specialty denial rate on first submission at roughly 8%, with more than half of organizations now seeing rates above 10%. Cardiology tends to run higher, and prior authorization is the biggest reason why: AMA's most recent physician survey found 93% of physicians say prior auth delays necessary care.
>
> The good news — cardiology denials sort into a small number of recurring patterns: delegated review vendors, medical necessity documentation gaps, modifier 26/TC errors, and frequency/bundling edits. Each has a different fix. Treating all of them with the same appeal template is why the denial rate never quite settles down.
>
> This applies whether you're a solo cardiologist or a multi-site group — the patterns are the same, just the volume differs.
>
> Full breakdown, payer by payer → https://medbpo360.com/blog/denial-management-cardiology-payer-patterns
>
> #medicalbilling #revenuecyclemanagement #denialmanagement #cardiology

### 2. Behavioral health denial rates

*Pillar: Billing & Denials*

> Behavioral health billing carries structural friction most specialties don't deal with.
>
> Care is frequently carved out to a separate managed behavioral health organization — bill the medical side instead and the claim denies correctly, because the carve-out entity was the right payer all along. Add in authorization rules that, despite federal parity law, have often run stricter than the medical/surgical side — the DOL's most recent MHPAEA report found corrections benefited more than 7.6 million plan participants — and it's clear why behavioral health denial rates sit above an already-elevated industry baseline.
>
> None of this is a documentation problem in disguise. It's routing, parity enforcement, and session-limit tracking — three different fixes, not one.
>
> How we sort the pattern → https://medbpo360.com/blog/behavioral-health-billing-denial-rates
>
> #medicalbilling #denialmanagement #behavioralhealth #healthcareadmin

### 3. Primary care: when to stop doing billing in-house

*Pillar: Billing & Denials*

> In-house billing works for primary care practices — until the regulatory load quietly outgrows it.
>
> MGMA's 2026 Regulatory Burden Report (230+ practice leaders surveyed) found nearly 95% reported rising regulatory burden over the past three years, and 40% are now hiring multiple full-time admin staff per physician just to keep up with prior auth, audits, and reporting.
>
> The tipping point usually isn't one crisis — it's a few signs showing up together: no one's auditing E/M coding anymore, AR days are climbing without a clear reason, and the same staff handling billing are also absorbing more prior-auth calls and appeals than they used to.
>
> This applies to a practice that's stayed the same size for years just as much as one that's actively adding providers — the burden scales with payer complexity, not growth ambition.
>
> Full read → https://medbpo360.com/blog/primary-care-billing-when-to-stop-in-house
>

### 4. Lab & diagnostics billing challenges

*Pillar: Billing & Denials*

> Lab billing isn't "physician billing but for tests" — it runs on a genuinely different regulatory framework.
>
> An OIG report published this January found Medicare Part B spending on clinical diagnostic lab tests rose 5% to $8.4 billion in 2024, while the number of enrollees receiving tests actually declined. Genetic testing accounted for 43% of that spending on 5% of the tests. That disproportion is why OIG's 2026 work plan added initiatives on genetic testing oversight and on expanded panels — those detecting six or more pathogens versus targeted panels detecting up to five.
>
> But enforcement attention isn't where most labs quietly lose money. It's the narrower rules: which date of service applies to a molecular test ordered on a hospital outpatient, whether your ABNs would survive review or read as routine, and whether coverage logic is keyed to the right MAC for each referring location.
>
> We put together a checklist of seven places diagnostic revenue leaks, with the CMS, OIG, and DOJ sources cited throughout so you can check any of it yourself. Worth running at any test volume.
>

### 5. Signs your in-house call center can't keep up

*Pillar: Call Center & Operations*

> Phone access degrades quietly — and it's rarely about dropped calls.
>
> A peer-reviewed VHA study (252,000+ patients, 285 facilities) found longer average speed of answer was linked to significantly worse patient-reported access — patients at the slowest-answering facilities were notably less likely to say they could get urgent appointments when they needed them. Abandonment rate itself wasn't the driver. How long it takes to get through is what patients actually remember.
>
> Growth doesn't break phone coverage all at once. It erodes it: the same staff covering check-in, phones, and billing questions simultaneously, average speed of answer creeping up, and nobody tracking it until it shows up as complaints — or patients who just stop calling back.
>
> Signs it's become a structural bottleneck, not a rough patch → https://medbpo360.com/blog/signs-in-house-call-center-cant-keep-up
>
> #patientexperience #healthcareoperations #practicemanagement #callcenter

### 6. Does a small practice need a call center partner?

*Pillar: Call Center & Operations*

> "Call center" sounds like an enterprise concept. For a small practice, the phone problem is often worse, not smaller.
>
> There's no redundancy — when the one front-desk person is out, calls just don't get answered. Layer in that MGMA found 40% of practices are now hiring multiple full-time admin staff per physician just to manage payer requirements, and that load competes directly with phone coverage for the same limited hours.
>
> A few signs it's time: patients have mentioned they couldn't get through more than once, front-desk turnover keeps resetting coverage quality to zero, and voicemails aren't reliably getting closed out.
>
> Redundancy a 3-provider practice doesn't otherwise have → https://medbpo360.com/blog/small-practice-call-center-partner

### 7. CAQH, PECOS, and state Medicaid credentialing roadmap

*Pillar: Credentialing*

> "Credentialing" sounds like one process. It's actually three — CAQH, Medicare's PECOS, and state Medicaid — each on its own timeline.
>
> Palmetto GBA (a CMS Medicare Administrative Contractor) reports a complete PECOS submission averages 7 days to process — an incomplete one jumps to 35. That's a 5x difference driven entirely by whether the paperwork was right the first time. CAQH attestation has to stay current on a recurring basis, not just at initial setup. And state Medicaid is frequently the slowest of the three, with no national benchmark since every state runs its own system.
>
> The fix: run all three in parallel, start CAQH first, submit PECOS complete, and start Medicaid as early as possible.
>
> Full roadmap → https://medbpo360.com/blog/caqh-pecos-medicaid-credentialing-roadmap
>
> #credentialing #payerenrollment #medicalbilling #healthcareadmin

### 8. Credentialing timelines when acquiring a new location

*Pillar: Credentialing*

> Acquiring or opening a new location resets the credentialing clock for every provider there, with every payer — even ones they're already credentialed with elsewhere.
>
> Medicare enrollment through PECOS commonly runs 60-90 days from a complete submission — and that clock starts when the paperwork is filed correctly, not when the deal closes. The most common (and most avoidable) mistake: modeling the financial timeline around the clinical/operational transition date instead of the credentialing timeline, which creates a foreseeable revenue gap between "clinically ready" and "actually billable."
>
> Starting credentialing the moment a deal is in motion — not after close — is what closes that gap.
>
> Full breakdown → https://medbpo360.com/blog/credentialing-timelines-acquiring-practice-location
>
> #credentialing #payerenrollment #practicemanagement #healthcareadmin

### 9. What full-cycle RCM actually covers

*Pillar: Billing & Denials*

> "RCM" gets used as a catch-all term. It's actually a specific, sequential set of functions — worth pinning down before deciding what to keep in-house.
>
> Front-end (eligibility, prior auth, registration) is where errors are hardest to catch later. Mid-cycle is coding and claim scrubbing — the stage where specialty-specific depth matters most. Back-end is submission, posting, and denial management, which is often where revenue leaks not because claims were coded wrong, but because a denied claim never gets worked. And reporting — clean claim rate, AR days, denial rate by payer/CPT — is the layer most practices lack entirely.
>
> Most practices keep front-desk registration in-house and hand off the rest. There's no single right line — it depends on where your actual gaps are.
>
> Full breakdown of each stage → https://medbpo360.com/blog/what-full-cycle-rcm-covers
>
> #revenuecyclemanagement #medicalbilling #healthcarefinance #practicemanagement

### 10. What to outsource first when back-office work outpaces staff

*Pillar: Industry Insights*

> When back-office work outpaces staff, the instinct is all-or-nothing — keep everything in-house, or hand off the whole thing. Neither is usually right.
>
> Back-office functions degrade unevenly. Claims processing is usually first to back up — high-volume, well-defined, and the most visible symptom of strain. Denial management often has the clearest ROI to outsource first, since an unworked denial is money already earned and at risk of write-off. Admin/data-entry work scales with volume regardless of staffing. And relationship-dependent work — patient continuity, provider-specific scheduling nuance — is usually the last thing to hand off, for good reason.
>
> The real question isn't "should we outsource" — it's which function is furthest behind right now.
>
> Full sequencing guide → https://medbpo360.com/blog/what-to-outsource-first-back-office
>
> #healthcareadmin #practicemanagement #outsourcing #medicalpractice

### 11. Billing across multiple specialties under one roof

*Pillar: Billing & Denials*

> A practice that adds a second specialty under one roof usually assumes the billing complexity scales down with size. It doesn't.
>
> A two-specialty practice runs into essentially the same coding-complexity problem a ten-specialty group does — just with less volume to absorb the cost of getting it wrong. Primary care E/M coding, procedural coding for something like cardiology, and behavioral health's carve-out and session-limit rules aren't variations on a theme — they're separate coding disciplines. A biller who's excellent at one may simply not have the depth for another.
>
> It also creates a coordination problem most systems don't catch: when the same patient sees two specialists in-house, avoiding duplicate E/M billing requires cross-departmental awareness that a lot of billing setups don't have. And a single dashboard that treats a behavioral health session the same as a cardiology procedure hides more than it shows.
>
> None of this requires a dedicated billing department per specialty — it requires specialty-specific expertise applied within one standardized operation.
>
> Full breakdown → https://medbpo360.com/blog/multi-specialty-billing-complexity
>
> #medicalbilling #revenuecyclemanagement #multispecialty #practicemanagement

### 6d. Measure, then adjust

After ~6 posts (roughly 2026-09-04), pull the page's Analytics → Updates tab and look at impressions and click-through per post. The three things worth acting on: which pillar gets the most engagement, what time of day performs, and whether posts with the link in the body underperform posts with the link in the first comment. Don't take either side on faith; run 3 posts each way and read your own numbers.

### 8b. The follower problem — harder here than on LinkedIn

**Constraint, stated by the owner 2026-08-17: they are not personally active on Facebook and will not invite friends.** That removes the single most effective free lever, because Facebook's "Invite friends" shows the recipient who invited them — which is both a personal-network ask and a nudge against the anonymity goal.

Be honest about what that leaves. Facebook suppresses organic Page reach hard, and a Page starting from zero followers with no invites and no spend grows *very* slowly. The realistic options:

1. **Owned properties — free, no personal exposure.** The website footer is done. Still available: email signature, the Clutch and GoodFirms listings (both live, both allow social links), the HBMA listing when it goes through, and a Google Business Profile.
2. **Paid — the only reliable lever that needs no personal network.** A modest Meta ads budget targeting geography plus healthcare-admin interests is the standard answer for a B2B Page starting cold. This is a spend decision, not a content decision.
3. **Accept low reach and treat the Page as a credibility surface.** This is the honest default, and it is not a failure mode. Someone who hears the company name and searches it finds a live, current, professional Page. That has real value *at zero followers*, and it's the main reason the posting queue is still worth running.

**What doesn't work here:** commenting as the Page in Groups. Most practice-management Groups require a personal profile to join, and Facebook restricts Page participation. The §6c "engage as the page" tactic is a LinkedIn play; it does not port.

**Recommendation:** run option 1 to exhaustion since it's free, treat option 3 as the Page's actual job, and revisit option 2 only if Facebook ever needs to be a lead source rather than a credibility check.
