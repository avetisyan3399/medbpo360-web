## 12. Screening colonoscopy modifiers — Thu 2026-09-17

> About a quarter of screening colonoscopies stop being screening colonoscopies partway through.
>
> A peer-reviewed analysis of screening colonoscopies in patients aged 50–75 found adjusted polypectomy rates between 22.6% and 26.2%, depending on how "screening" was defined. So roughly one in four times, the procedure that was scheduled and the claim that actually needs billing are two different things by the time the patient is in recovery.
>
> Here is where it gets expensive: the modifier depends on who is paying. Medicare wants modifier PT. Commercial plans want modifier 33. They are not interchangeable — and a biller working from one mental model of "how we code a converted colonoscopy" gets roughly half of them wrong by definition.
>
> There is a second trap underneath it. Even coded correctly, Medicare beneficiaries still owe a share when a polyp is removed during a screening: 15% through 2026, 10% from 2027 through 2029, full coverage only in 2030. A practice that assumes the ACA's zero-cost-sharing preventive rule applies identically across Medicare and commercial plans will bill a patient who was promised a free screening.
>
> Neither of these is fixable after the denial. Both are decided at coding.
>
> Full breakdown → https://medbpo360.com/blog/gastroenterology-screening-colonoscopy-billing

## 13. Independent practice trend — Mon 2026-09-21

> The number gets repeated constantly, usually by someone selling an exit: physicians in private practice fell from 60.1% in 2012 to 42.2% in 2024, per the AMA's Physician Practice Benchmark Survey.
>
> It is real data and a real trend. It is also routinely used to argue independent practice is a dead end, which is not what the data says.
>
> What the same survey shows: hospital-owned practices grew from 23.4% to 34.5% of physicians, direct hospital employment from 5.6% to 12.2%, and private equity ownership from 4.5% in 2022 to 6.5% in 2024. Practices of 10 or fewer physicians fell from 61.4% to 47.4% of doctors.
>
> Read it carefully and 42.2% of physicians are still in private practice after twelve years of consolidation pressure. That is not a vanishing category. It is a large, durable one.
>
> What did change is the operational floor. Staying independent now means matching systems that larger organisations build deliberately — credentialing that does not lapse, denials that get worked, reporting that shows where revenue actually goes. No solo or small practice builds those by accident, and nobody has to grow to get them.
>
> Full read → https://medbpo360.com/blog/independent-practice-consolidation-trends
>

## 14. ASC per-case risk — Thu 2026-09-24

> Medicare is steering surgical volume toward ambulatory surgery centers because the savings are documented, not theoretical.
>
> ASCs generated an estimated $27.9 billion in Medicare savings between 2019 and 2024, and in 2024 Medicare paid ASCs on average just 62% of what it pays hospital outpatient departments for the same procedures. More than 6,300 Medicare-certified ASCs now perform roughly 7 million procedures a year for Medicare beneficiaries.
>
> The shift is furthest along where you would expect: as of 2023, over 70% of eligible eye and ocular procedures and roughly 40% of eligible digestive and nervous system procedures were already happening in ASCs rather than hospital outpatient departments.
>
> That same economics is the reason a single mistake costs more here. When the facility payment is 62% of the hospital rate, there is materially less margin to absorb a miscoded implant, an expired authorization, or an out-of-network anesthesia claim. The error is the same size; the buffer is smaller.
>
