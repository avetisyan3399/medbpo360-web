## #12 — Screening colonoscopy modifiers
*Scheduled: Thu 2026-10-01*

```
About one in four screening colonoscopies stops being a screening colonoscopy partway through.

A peer-reviewed analysis of patients aged 50-75 found that between 22.6% and 26.2% had a polyp removed, depending on how "screening" was defined. So roughly a quarter of the time, the procedure that got scheduled and the claim that actually has to be billed are two different things by the time the patient is in recovery.

Here's the expensive part: the right code depends on who's paying. Medicare wants modifier PT. Commercial plans want modifier 33. They aren't interchangeable, and a biller working from one mental model of "how we code these" gets about half of them wrong by definition.

There's a second trap underneath it. Even coded correctly, Medicare patients still owe a share when a polyp comes out during a screening — 15% through 2026, 10% from 2027 through 2029, and full coverage only in 2030. A practice that assumes "preventive means free" across every plan will bill someone who was promised a free screening.

Neither one is fixable after the denial. Both are decided at coding.

## #13 — Independent practice trend
*Scheduled: Tue 2026-10-06*

```
"Private practice is dying." You've heard it — usually from someone selling an exit.

The number behind it is real. Physicians in private practice fell from 60.1% in 2012 to 42.2% in 2024, according to the American Medical Association's Physician Practice Benchmark Survey.

But read it again. After twelve straight years of consolidation pressure, 42.2% of physicians are still in private practice. That isn't a vanishing category. It's a large, durable one.

What actually changed isn't whether you can stay independent. It's the operational floor for doing it. Staying independent now means running the systems bigger organizations build on purpose: credentialing that doesn't lapse, denials that actually get worked, reporting that shows where the revenue went.

No small practice builds those by accident. And nobody has to grow to get them.

https://medbpo360.com/blog/independent-practice-consolidation-trends

## #14 — ASC per-case risk
*Scheduled: Thu 2026-10-08*

```
Medicare is steering surgery out of hospitals and into surgery centers, and the savings are documented rather than theoretical.

Ambulatory surgery centers generated an estimated $27.9 billion in Medicare savings between 2019 and 2024. In 2024, Medicare paid those centers an average of 62% of what it pays a hospital outpatient department for the same procedure.

That's excellent for Medicare. It's also exactly why one billing mistake costs a surgery center more than it costs a hospital.

When the facility payment is 62% of the hospital rate, there's far less margin to absorb a miscoded implant, an expired authorization, or an out-of-network anesthesia claim. The error is the same size. The cushion is smaller.

## #15 — Health system denial patterns
*Scheduled: Tue 2026-10-13*

```
Hospital claim denials got worse again in 2025 — and the growth is concentrated in the category that's hardest to fix.

Kodiak Solutions analyzed roughly 2,300 hospitals and 350,000 physicians. Average initial denial rates rose to 11.6% from 11.4%, median final denial rates climbed from 2.5% to 2.7%, and net revenue leakage jumped 25% year over year. Those hospitals collectively missed $48.4 billion in 2025, up from $38.6 billion.

The composition matters more than the headline. Clinical denials — prior authorization failures and medical necessity determinations — rose from 2.4% to 2.6%. Denials where the payer simply wants more information went from 3.4% to 3.6%.

That difference decides where the money should go. Clerical denials get solved with better claim scrubbing. Clinical denials need authorization workflows, documentation standards, and payer-specific appeals — different work, aimed at a different part of the organization.

At scale there's a quieter problem underneath: the same fixable pattern can be running in five departments at once with nobody connecting them, because each one only sees its own numbers.

https://medbpo360.com/blog/health-system-denial-patterns-across-facilities

## #16 — Multi-site revenue variance
*Scheduled: Thu 2026-10-15*

```
When one location collects better than another, everyone blames patient mix. It usually isn't patient mix.

Experian Health surveyed 250 healthcare professionals for its State of Claims 2025 report. Incomplete or inaccurate information collected during check-in is now the third most common cause of denials, and 26% of respondents said at least one in ten of their denials traces back to intake errors.

Check-in is exactly the process that varies most between sites. Same group, same payers, same specialties, different front desk. Then demographics get blamed for a gap that's really about who has time to verify eligibility carefully and who is covering three jobs at once.

The test is simple: compare denial rates by reason code across your locations instead of looking at collections in aggregate. If one site's eligibility and registration denials run higher, that's a staffing and training answer, not a market one.

True at two locations as much as at twenty. Fewer sites just means less data to see the pattern in.

https://medbpo360.com/blog/multi-site-groups-revenue-variance-by-location

## #17 — MSO/PE acquisition onboarding
*Scheduled: Tue 2026-10-20*

```
In a healthcare roll-up, the thing that compounds isn't any individual deal. It's the onboarding process.

The Private Equity Stakeholder Project tracked 1,029 private equity healthcare transactions in 2025. Of those, 664 were add-on acquisitions — practices folded into an existing platform — against 151 leveraged buyouts and 214 growth investments.

Add-ons dominating that mix changes the operational question. Underwriting one practice well is a deal skill. Integrating the twelfth one as cleanly as the second is an operations skill, and it's the one that decides platform returns.

Where it degrades is predictable. Credentialing starts after close instead of when the deal is in motion, so providers are clinically live and unbillable. Each acquired practice keeps its own billing conventions, because standardizing them was nobody's line item. And reporting can't compare sites because no two define the same metric identically.

None of that shows up in the first acquisition. All of it shows up by the fifth.

https://medbpo360.com/blog/mso-pe-acquisition-onboarding-billing

#privateequity #revenuecyclemanagement

## #18 — Hospital-based out-of-network disputes
*Scheduled: Thu 2026-10-22*

```
Emergency, radiology, and anesthesia groups didn't choose to sit at the center of the surprise billing dispute system. They're there structurally, because their patients don't select them.

The federal Independent Dispute Resolution data shows the scale: 1,243,621 disputes involving emergency and non-emergency services were initiated between January 2023 and June 2024, and nearly two in three disputed services involved care furnished in an emergency room.

Here's the part most groups underestimate. Providers and facilities initiated 90% of those disputes and prevailed 80% of the time — and the provider win rate climbed from 68% in early 2023 to 85% by early 2024.

That's a very high success rate on a process most groups treat as a last resort. If disputes are usually won, the constraint isn't whether to pursue them. It's whether the group has the operational capacity to file within the deadlines, with the documentation the process requires, at volume.

For a hospital-based group, that makes billing operations a strategic function rather than a back-office one.

https://medbpo360.com/blog/hospital-based-groups-out-of-network-disputes

#revenuecyclemanagement #emergencymedicine
```
