## 1. Cardiology denial patterns — Thu 2026-08-20

> Cardiology claim denials aren't random. They fall into a handful of repeating patterns.
>
> Industry benchmarking from MGMA puts the average first-submission denial rate at around 8%, with more than half of organizations now seeing rates above 10%. Cardiology tends to run higher, and prior authorization is the biggest reason why — in the AMA's most recent physician survey, 93% of physicians said prior auth delays necessary care.
>
> The useful part: cardiology denials sort into a small number of recurring causes. Prior auth routed through a third-party review vendor. Documentation that doesn't support medical necessity. Professional vs. technical component billing on diagnostic studies. And rules about how often a service can be billed, or which services get bundled together.
>
> Each one needs a different fix. Sending the same appeal letter at all of them is why the denial rate never quite settles down.
>
> Same patterns whether you're a solo cardiologist or a multi-site group. Only the volume changes.
>
> Full breakdown, payer by payer:
> https://medbpo360.com/blog/denial-management-cardiology-payer-patterns

## 2. Behavioral health denial rates — Tue 2026-08-25

> Behavioral health billing has a problem most specialties don't: the claim can be perfect and still deny.
>
> Care is often "carved out" to a separate behavioral health plan. Bill the medical side instead and the denial is actually correct — the carve-out plan was the right payer all along.
>
> Add authorization rules that, despite federal parity law, have often run stricter than the medical side. The Department of Labor's most recent parity report found corrections that benefited more than 7.6 million plan participants.
>
> None of this is a documentation problem in disguise. It's three separate issues — which payer, parity enforcement, and session-limit tracking — and they need three different fixes.
>
> How we sort the pattern:
> https://medbpo360.com/blog/behavioral-health-billing-denial-rates
>

## 3. Primary care: when to stop billing in-house — Thu 2026-08-27

> In-house billing works for primary care — right up until the paperwork quietly outgrows it.
>
> MGMA's 2026 Regulatory Burden Report surveyed more than 230 practice leaders. Nearly 95% said regulatory burden has risen over the past three years, and 40% are now hiring multiple full-time admin staff per physician just to keep up with prior authorizations, audits, and reporting.
>
> The tipping point usually isn't one crisis. It's a few things showing up together: nobody's checking the coding anymore, the money's taking longer to come in without a clear reason, and the same people handling billing are also fielding more prior-auth calls and appeals than they used to.
>
> This hits a practice that's been the same size for a decade just as hard as one adding providers. The burden scales with payer complexity, not ambition.
>
> Full read:
> https://medbpo360.com/blog/primary-care-billing-when-to-stop-in-house

## 4. Lab & diagnostics billing — Tue 2026-09-01

> Lab billing isn't "regular billing but for tests." It runs on a different rulebook entirely.
>
> Federal price reporting requirements. Coverage rules that vary by which Medicare contractor handles your region — so the same test can be covered in one state and denied in another. Advance Beneficiary Notices that only hold up if they're issued correctly, not handed out as a blanket habit. And molecular and toxicology testing, which draws sustained federal scrutiny.
>
> Each is a separate obligation with its own way of failing. A lab can be excellent at price reporting and still have a live coverage-rule gap it doesn't know about.
>
> Worth auditing at any test volume, not just at scale.
>
> Where the gaps usually hide:
> https://medbpo360.com/blog/labs-diagnostics-billing-challenges

## 5. Signs your call center can't keep up — Thu 2026-09-03

> Phone access falls apart quietly. It's rarely about dropped calls.
>
> A peer-reviewed study of more than 252,000 patients across 285 VA facilities found that how long it took to answer the phone was linked to significantly worse patient-reported access. Patients at the slowest-answering facilities were notably less likely to say they could get an urgent appointment when they needed one. The abandonment rate wasn't what drove it — the wait to get through was.
>
> Growth doesn't break phone coverage all at once. It erodes it. The same person covers check-in, phones, and billing questions at once, hold times creep up, and nobody's tracking it until it surfaces as complaints — or as patients who just stop calling back.
>
> Signs it's structural, not a rough patch:
> https://medbpo360.com/blog/signs-in-house-call-center-cant-keep-up
>

## 6. Does a small practice need a call center partner? — Tue 2026-09-08

> "Call center" sounds like a big-practice thing. For a small practice, the phone problem is usually worse, not smaller.
>
> There's no backup. When the one front-desk person is out sick, calls simply don't get answered. And MGMA found 40% of practices are now hiring multiple full-time admin staff per physician just to manage payer requirements — work that competes directly with answering the phone for the same limited hours.
>
> A few signs it's time: patients have mentioned more than once that they couldn't get through, front-desk turnover keeps resetting coverage quality to zero, and voicemails aren't reliably getting returned.

## 7. CAQH, Medicare, and Medicaid credentialing roadmap — Thu 2026-09-10

> "Credentialing" sounds like one process. It's three, each on its own clock.
>
> CAQH, Medicare enrollment, and state Medicaid all run separately. Palmetto GBA — one of Medicare's regional contractors — reports that a complete Medicare enrollment averages 7 days to process. An incomplete one jumps to 35. That's a five-fold difference decided entirely by whether the paperwork was right the first time.
>
> CAQH also has to be re-attested on a recurring basis, not just set up once. And state Medicaid is usually the slowest of the three, with no national benchmark, because every state runs its own system.
>
> The fix is boring and it works: run all three in parallel, start CAQH first, submit Medicare complete, and start Medicaid as early as you can.
>
> Full roadmap:
> https://medbpo360.com/blog/caqh-pecos-medicaid-credentialing-roadmap
>

## 8. Credentialing timelines for a new location — Tue 2026-09-15

> Opening or acquiring a new location restarts credentialing for every provider there, with every payer — even the ones they're already credentialed with somewhere else.
>
> Medicare enrollment commonly runs 60 to 90 days from a complete submission. That clock starts when the paperwork is filed correctly, not when the deal closes.
>
> The most common and most avoidable mistake: building the financial plan around the date the location opens clinically, instead of the date it can actually bill. That gap between "seeing patients" and "getting paid" is entirely foreseeable.
>
> Starting credentialing the moment a deal is in motion — not after closing — is what closes it.
>
> Full breakdown:

## 9. What full-cycle RCM actually covers — Thu 2026-09-17

> "RCM" gets used as a catch-all. It's actually a specific sequence — worth pinning down before you decide what to keep in-house.
>
> The front end is eligibility, prior auth, and registration; errors there are the hardest to catch later. The middle is coding and claim scrubbing, where specialty-specific depth matters most. The back end is submission, payment posting, and denial management — which is where revenue usually leaks, not because claims were coded wrong, but because a denied claim never got worked.
>
> Then reporting: clean claim rate, days in AR, denial rate by payer and by code. That's the layer most practices don't have at all.
>
> Most practices keep front-desk registration in-house and hand off the rest. There's no single right line — it depends on where your actual gaps are.
>
> Full breakdown of each stage:
> https://medbpo360.com/blog/what-full-cycle-rcm-covers
>

## 10. What to outsource first — Tue 2026-09-22

> When back-office work outpaces staff, the instinct is all-or-nothing: keep everything, or hand off everything. Neither is usually right.
>
> Back-office work doesn't degrade evenly. Claims processing is usually first to back up — high volume, well-defined, and the most visible sign of strain. Denial management often has the clearest payoff to hand off first, since an unworked denial is money you already earned and are about to write off. Admin and data entry scale with volume no matter how you're staffed.
>
> And the relationship-dependent work — patient continuity, the scheduling quirks of a specific provider — is usually the last thing to hand off, for good reason.
>
> The real question isn't "should we outsource." It's which function is furthest behind right now.
>

## 11. Billing across multiple specialties — Thu 2026-09-24

> Add a second specialty under one roof and the billing complexity doesn't scale down with your size. It arrives all at once.
>
> A two-specialty practice hits essentially the same coding problem a ten-specialty group does — just with less volume to absorb the cost of getting it wrong. Primary care visit coding, procedural coding for something like cardiology, and behavioral health's carve-out and session-limit rules aren't variations on a theme. They're separate disciplines. A biller who's excellent at one may simply not have the depth for another.
>
> It also creates a coordination problem most systems miss: when the same patient sees two of your specialists, avoiding duplicate visit billing takes cross-department awareness a lot of billing setups don't have. And one dashboard that treats a therapy session the same as a cardiology procedure hides more than it shows.
>
> None of this needs a separate billing department per specialty. It needs specialty-specific expertise inside one standardized operation.
>
> Full breakdown:
