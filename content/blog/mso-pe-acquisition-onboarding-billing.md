The shape of private equity healthcare activity says something useful about what platforms actually need operationally. The Private Equity Stakeholder Project tracked 1,029 private equity healthcare transactions in 2025 — and of those, 664 were add-on acquisitions, against 151 leveraged buyouts and 214 growth investments ([Private Equity Stakeholder Project](https://pestakeholder.org/reports/pe-healthcare-deals-2025-in-review/), compiled from PitchBook and LevinPro HC data). Add-ons outnumber new platform formations by better than four to one.

## Add-on-heavy dealmaking makes integration a repeated process, not a project

A platform that will acquire a dozen practices over a hold period doesn't need a good integration once. It needs the same integration twelve times, executed by people who won't all be the same people, at a consistency level that doesn't decay as the platform gets busier. That's a fundamentally different operational requirement than doing one careful conversion — and it's the requirement most platforms underestimate, because the first one or two acquisitions usually go fine on attention and effort alone.

## Every acquired practice arrives with its own billing maturity

One target has a tenured biller who knows every payer rep by name and keeps a denial log in a spreadsheet nobody else can read. The next outsourced billing years ago and has no institutional knowledge left in-house at all. A third has been coding conservatively for a decade and is leaving money on the table in a way that looks like clean performance until someone audits it. Standardizing all three onto platform workflows without a cash-flow dip during transition is the actual work — and "we'll figure out each one as it comes" is how a platform ends up with twelve billing operations wearing one logo.

## Credentialing timing is where deal value quietly slips

A newly acquired provider who isn't yet enrolled under the platform's payer contracts can't bill at the platform's negotiated rates. Every week of lag between close and enrollment is a week the acquired practice is producing revenue at its old economics rather than the ones the deal was underwritten on. That gap doesn't show up as a failure — the practice is still billing, claims are still paying — which is exactly why it goes unnoticed until someone reconciles realized synergy against the model.

## Portfolio reporting has to be comparable, not just present

Boards and diligence on the next add-on both need RCM metrics that mean the same thing across every practice in the portfolio. If days-in-AR is calculated one way at three practices and another way at the rest, the portfolio view isn't a view — it's an average of incompatible numbers. Getting definitions standardized at onboarding, rather than reconciling them at reporting time, is the difference between a metric leadership can act on and a number that starts an argument.
