Hospital-based physician groups ended up structurally exposed to the No Surprises Act in a way almost no other provider type did. Anesthesiologists, emergency physicians, and radiologists routinely treat patients who chose the hospital, not the physician — which is precisely the scenario the law was written to address. The result is visible in the federal Independent Dispute Resolution data: 1,243,621 disputes involving emergency and non-emergency services were initiated through the federal IDR process between January 2023 and June 2024, and nearly two in three disputed services involved care furnished in an emergency room ([Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/brief/the-performance-of-the-federal-independent-dispute-resolution-process-through-mid-2024/), analyzing CMS IDR reports).

## The dispute volume is concentrated in exactly these specialties

Emergency department services, radiology, and anesthesia account for the bulk of federal IDR activity. That isn't a reflection of how these groups bill — it's a reflection of where out-of-network situations structurally arise in American healthcare delivery. A hospitalist group or anesthesia group can run a completely disciplined billing operation and still find itself in the dispute system at volume, simply because of the setting it practices in.

## Providers initiate most disputes and win most of them — but that's an operational cost, not free money

Across the period studied, providers and facilities initiated 90% of disputes and prevailed 80% of the time, with the provider win rate climbing from 68% in early 2023 to 85% by early 2024 ([Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/brief/the-performance-of-the-federal-independent-dispute-resolution-process-through-mid-2024/)). A high win rate is genuinely good news for hospital-based groups. But every one of those wins required someone to identify the dispute as worth initiating, assemble the supporting information, meet the deadlines, and pay the administrative fee — and 43% of disputes had reached a payment determination at all, with 41% still outstanding. Cash tied up in an adjudication queue is cash the group can't use, no matter how favorable the eventual ruling.

## Meanwhile the underlying coordination risk hasn't gone anywhere

The IDR exposure sits on top of the coordination problem hospital-based groups already had: professional fee billing running in parallel with the facility's technical billing. Billing a global fee when the facility has already billed the technical component isn't just a revenue error — it's compliance exposure. Modifier 26 discipline, and knowing precisely where the professional/technical boundary falls for each service and each facility arrangement, remains foundational regardless of what's happening in the dispute system.

## Thin per-case margins mean process efficiency compounds

Anesthesia and hospitalist billing in particular run on high case volume with modest margin per case. At that structure, a small inefficiency — a percentage point of clean-claim rate, a few days of avoidable AR, a dispute-eligible claim that nobody flagged in time — compounds into meaningful money across a year's case volume. The same inefficiency at a low-volume, high-dollar practice would be an annoyance; here it's material.

## What this adds up to

For hospital-based groups, billing operations aren't a back-office function that can be run on autopilot. Between IDR participation, professional/facility coordination, rotating-roster credentialing across multiple facilities, and high-volume case-based processing, the billing operation is closer to a strategic capability — and it needs to be staffed and measured like one.
