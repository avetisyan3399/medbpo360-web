A group with four locations will usually have one that collects noticeably better than the others. The intuitive explanation is that the sites serve different populations — different payer mix, different demographics, different acuity. Sometimes that's genuinely true. But it's worth ruling out the less flattering explanation first, because it's both more common and more fixable: the sites are executing billing differently, and nobody has the site-level reporting to see it.

## Intake errors are a growing denial category, not a solved one

Experian Health's State of Claims 2025 survey of 250 healthcare professionals found that incomplete or inaccurate information collected during check-in is now the third most common cause of denials, with 26% of respondents reporting that at least one in ten denials at their organization traces back to intake errors ([Experian Health](https://www.experian.com/blogs/healthcare/state-of-claims-2025/)). The broader trend in the same survey is going the wrong way too — 41% of providers now say more than 10% of their claims are denied, up from 38% in 2024 and 30% in 2022, and 54% say claim errors are increasing year over year.

## Front-desk execution is exactly the thing that drifts by location

Eligibility verification, benefit checks, collecting the right secondary insurance information, confirming demographics haven't changed since the last visit — these are habits, not systems, unless someone makes them systems. And habits are local. A location with a long-tenured front desk that verifies eligibility every visit will out-collect an otherwise identical location where verification happens when things are slow. Same patients, same payers, same providers, different results — and the difference doesn't show up as "our verification discipline varies by site." It shows up as unexplained AR variance months later.

## The reporting has to be site-level or the variance stays invisible

A group-wide denial rate averages the good sites and the struggling ones into a single number that describes neither. Segmenting denial rate, days in AR, and denial reason by location is what turns "collections are soft this quarter" into "the Northside location's eligibility-related denials are running triple everyone else's, and it started when the front desk turned over." One of those is a budget problem. The other is a training problem with a specific address, and it can be fixed in a few weeks.

## New locations inherit whatever wasn't standardized

The same gap compounds at opening. Without a documented playbook covering credentialing sequencing, payer enrollment, eligibility workflows, and collections scripts, every new site relearns what the last one learned — usually by making the same mistakes and absorbing the same months of slow collections while it sorts them out. That's a repeatable, avoidable cost, and it's entirely a function of whether the group treats site setup as a process or as a project someone figures out each time.
